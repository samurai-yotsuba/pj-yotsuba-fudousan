**In short:** A pharmacy's labor foundation is arranging shifts so the managing pharmacist can genuinely be full-time and dedicated, managing the working hours of pharmacists and registered sellers by law, and setting up social insurance early. A pharmacy is compulsorily covered for health and pension once a sole proprietorship regularly uses five or more staff, and a corporation is covered with even one owner. The opening license is a pharmaceutical-law procedure, handled separately from labor.

A dispensing pharmacy typically has the opener, a managing pharmacist, other pharmacists, and — if it also sells over-the-counter (OTC) drugs — registered sellers and clerical staff. In preparing to open, attention tends to go to premises, equipment, and the opening license, but "can we secure a full-time managing pharmacist?" and "can we run shifts without ever lacking a qualified person during opening hours?" tie directly into working-hour and social-insurance design. Pin down the labor foundation first.

## What labor points should you arrange first when opening a pharmacy?

The first three things to decide in a pharmacy's labor are as follows.

| Issue | What to decide |
|---|---|
| The managing pharmacist's hours | Whether you can secure enough hours to place them dedicated and full-time (Pharmaceutical and Medical Device Act, Article 7(4)) |
| Placement of qualified staff during opening hours | Whether you can build shifts that never lack a pharmacist or registered seller |
| Social and employment insurance coverage | When coverage obligations arise, both as a workplace and for each employee |

The pharmacy opening license (Pharmaceutical and Medical Device Act, Article 4, a prefectural governor's license) is a pharmaceutical procedure, and it also looks at whether the managing pharmacist has a work arrangement that lets them manage on site. Because the opening license and labor design overlap here, fixing working hours, shifts, and employment contracts in parallel with the license application reduces rework. Reading it alongside [labor at a medical clinic](/en/labor/column/clinic-kaigyo-roumu-shukunikkin-kangoshi-shakaihoken) and [labor at a dental clinic and dental lab](/en/labor/column/shika-iin-gikoushi-roumu-part-shakaihoken) shows the feel of medical-sector labor.

## How is the managing pharmacist's "full-time" status treated in labor?

If the pharmacy opener is a pharmacist, they manage the pharmacy on site themselves; if the opener is not a pharmacist, they designate a manager (managing pharmacist) from among the pharmacists engaged in pharmaceutical work there to manage it on site (Article 7(1) and (2)). And a managing pharmacist may not, as a rule, engage in pharmaceutical work at any place other than that pharmacy; concurrent work requires the prefectural governor's permission (Article 7(4)).

So the managing pharmacist is premised on being "dedicated to that pharmacy and working enough to manage it on site." Note that the pharmaceutical "dedicated, on-site management" and the labor/social-insurance sense of "full-time" are different yardsticks.

| Yardstick | Content |
|---|---|
| Pharmaceutical manager requirement | Concurrent work at another store is in principle prohibited (permitted by exception). Hours enough to manage the pharmacy on site (Article 7(4)) |
| Labor/social-insurance "full-time" | There is no fixed statutory number of hours; it usually means scheduled hours that are full-time (roughly the same as a regular employee) |

If you make the managing pharmacist work "only a few days a week," whether on-site management is actually happening comes into question. On the labor side, stating scheduled hours and workdays clearly in the employment contract and work rules, backed by actual attendance records, is safe for both pharmaceutical and labor purposes. Whether a specific concurrent arrangement or number of hours meets the manager requirement is a matter for the pharmaceutical licensing authority, so confirm it within the opening-license preparation.

## How do you build shifts and working hours for pharmacists and registered sellers?

During opening hours a pharmacy needs a pharmacist for dispensing, and a pharmacist or registered seller for OTC sales (second- and third-class drugs), so a shift design that never lacks a qualified person is essential. Working-hour management follows the Labor Standards Act's principles regardless of industry.

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Statutory working hours | In principle 8 hours a day and 40 hours a week (Labor Standards Act, Article 32) |
| Breaks | At least 45 minutes for over 6 hours, at least 1 hour for over 8 hours (Article 34) |
| Overtime and holiday work | Cannot be ordered without an Article 36 agreement (Article 36) |
| Premium wages | Premium wages for overtime, late-night, and holiday work (Article 37) |

For pharmacies with long opening hours, or different hours on weekdays and Saturdays, a monthly-unit variable working-hours system (Article 32-2) makes it easier to allocate scheduled hours to weekly peaks and troughs. See [how to choose between the variable working-hours system and flextime](/en/labor/column/henkei-roudoujikan-flextime-erabikata). For part-time pharmacists and registered sellers working within a dependent's range, decide scheduled hours and workdays clearly, mindful of the social-insurance entry point below.

## From what point do social and employment insurance obligations arise?

First, confirm whether the pharmacy **is a workplace covered by social insurance (health insurance and employees' pension).** A pharmacy sells drugs — goods — and falls within the applicable industry (the business of selling goods) under the Health Insurance Act and Employees' Pension Insurance Act.

| Business form | Health insurance / employees' pension |
|---|---|
| Corporation (KK, medical corporation, etc.) | **Compulsory even with the owner alone** |
| Sole-proprietor pharmacy | **Compulsory with 5 or more regular staff** (selling goods is an applicable industry) |

Health Insurance Act Article 3(3) and Employees' Pension Insurance Act Article 6 make sole proprietorships regularly using five or more compulsorily covered, while excluding service industries such as restaurants and beauty/barber shops. Because a pharmacy is the business of selling goods, an applicable industry, a sole proprietorship with five or more regular staff is a compulsorily covered workplace — the opposite of a sole-proprietor beauty/barber shop, which is not compulsory even with five or more.

Once the workplace is covered, whether part-time pharmacists and registered sellers join is judged against regular employees.

| Test | Basis for joining |
|---|---|
| Three-quarters standard | Both weekly scheduled hours and monthly scheduled workdays are three-quarters or more of a regular employee's |
| Coverage expansion (specified covered workplace) | At workplaces with a certain number of insured persons, joining if 20+ hours a week, monthly wage of 88,000 yen or more, expected employment over 2 months, and not a student |

The size requirement is expanding in stages — 51 or more insured persons, and 36 or more from October 2027. The 88,000-yen wage requirement is scheduled to be abolished in October 2026. Counting is in [what happens to social insurance when you hire for short hours](/en/labor/column/tanjikan-koyo-shakaihoken-4bunno3), the timing in [social-insurance coverage expansion to 36+ from October 2027](/en/labor/column/shakaihoken-tekiyo-kakudai-2027), and explaining it to those working within a dependent's range in [what employers should do about abolishing the 1.06-million-yen wall](/en/labor/column/106man-no-kabe-teppai-jigyousha-yaru-koto). For employment insurance, someone with scheduled weekly hours of 20 or more (10 or more from October 2028) and expected employment of 31 days or more is insured. Work rules must be drawn up and filed from 10 or more regular staff (Article 89; [from how many people are work rules mandatory](/en/labor/column/shugyokisoku-10nin-gimu-nani-ga-hitsuyo)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Can the managing pharmacist work part-time?**
A. Under the pharmaceutical law, a managing pharmacist is premised on being able to manage the pharmacy on site, and is in principle prohibited from concurrent work at another store (permitted by exception with the prefectural governor's permission). With only a few short days a week, whether on-site management is happening comes into question, so the basic approach is to secure hours that allow a dedicated, full-time placement. How many hours meet the manager requirement is a matter for the pharmaceutical licensing authority, so confirm it within the opening-license preparation.

**Q. Our pharmacy is a sole proprietorship — is it really not exempt from social insurance even with five or more staff?**
A. No, you are covered. A pharmacy is the business of selling goods — drugs — so it falls within the applicable industry for health and pension, and a sole proprietorship with five or more regular staff is a compulsorily covered workplace. This is the opposite of service industries such as beauty/barber shops, which are not compulsory even with five or more, so be careful. A corporation is compulsory even with the owner alone.

**Q. I want a registered seller as a part-timer within a dependent's range — up to how many hours before they join social insurance?**
A. Compared with a regular employee, if both weekly scheduled hours and monthly scheduled workdays are under three-quarters, they in principle do not join. But if the workplace is a specified covered workplace, they become subject with conditions such as 20+ hours a week and a monthly wage of 88,000 yen or more. The size requirement is expanding in stages and the 88,000-yen wage requirement is scheduled to be abolished in October 2026, so judge by the latest requirements.

**Q. How should shifts be built at a pharmacy with long opening hours to avoid overtime?**
A. To have people work beyond 8 hours a day or 40 hours a week you need an Article 36 agreement filed, and premium wages apply to the excess. At a pharmacy with different weekday and Saturday hours, a monthly-unit variable working-hours system lets you allocate scheduled hours to weekly peaks and troughs and run shifts more smoothly. Making scheduled hours, breaks, and overtime treatment clear in work rules and attendance records prevents unpaid-overtime trouble.
